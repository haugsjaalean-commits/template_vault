## Thoughts

To me, Stenopaper is about one very specific thing: the mariage between the digital and the analogue. The question of marrying these two things has been at the center of UI design since the very beginning. At first, every button on the IPhone was made to look like a real button. Sometime after that, they opted for a much flatter look, prioritizing visually clarity and simplicity. Not so long ago, they introduced a new look, which gives buttons highlights without fully committing to making them look like real buttons. Along with this, we got a lot of UI elements with a glass-like look, seemingly mimicking the actually thing that the user is touching. This whole evolution fascinates me, as it seems to speak to a central question which is posed in the design of every user interface: "How *real* should this look?". There is no right answer to this question, but I think that it should be one which we put a lot of importance into, whenever designing any UI. 

I think that making things look real is a very powerful to for many reasons. When we make the edges of objects look like they are catching light, our eyes are naturally drawn to them. If we make something visibly in the foreground by mimicking the effects of 3D geometry, the same effect is achieved. However, beyond the simply functionality of realistic design, there is another, arguably more powerful advantage that comes with it: storytelling. 

> [!quote] [Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeuomorph)
> A [[Skeuomorphism|skeuomorph]] is a derivative object that retains ornamental [design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design "Design") cues (attributes) from structures that were necessary in the original.

Using skeuomorphisme, we can tell a story about what the interface does by referring back to well understood physical objects. A blank white page glaring at you from a computer screen can be a lot of things, but if you hint at the design of a typewriter, you immediately understand that that page is meant for writing. By using this design language, we can easily communicate things which would otherwise be difficult to get across; however, this is not the only advantage: we can also use skeuomorphs to immerse the user. Of course the Obsidian user will know that the open tab in front of them is meant for writing without the interface hinting at a typewriter, but will they feel like they are writing on a typewriter? No, obviously not. Maybe they do not even want to feel like they are writing on a typewriter, but I think that immersion is one of the most powerful and sought after things in any creation. The point of any design is to give the user a certain feeling. My goal with Stenopaper is to give the user a very intimate feeling of writing something that really *exists* - not just text on a screen. Some interfaces are meant to do as little story telling as possible in order to leave a completely blank canvas for the user, but I think that there is a lot of value in telling a story and immersing someone. Both options have value, but, for this style, I am choosing the latter. 

Interestingly, this debate about things looking real or telling the truth about what they are has been something I've been obsessed with since my early childhood. I really hated the idea that video games made up fake stories that they told you about what the character was. I wanted everything to be completely literal, and this desire was at the heart of many of my old video game ideas. Something felt wrong to me about an interface lying about what it actually was. I think that this goes back to a point made in [[Decoration, Function, Elegance]]. [[Aaron Vartiainen]] talks about how things that try to imitate beauty end of feeling very ugly, whereas things that simply do there job end of feeling elegant and there for beautiful. If I apply this to my long standing *ick* with fake looking interfaces, it stands to reason that I don't like them because the mind is not fooled, and it knows that they are selling a lie; however, I did not completely agree with Aaron. He said that it is impossible to make something look elegant by "decorating" it. I do not agree with this. I don't agree because I am an artist and I make drawings, and very few of them serve a purpose, and a very good amount of them are elegant. I am good at making elegant yet useless things. To me, this means that elegance can be imitated, but not without tact. I think that creating elegance with tact means understanding the context of the creation. In video games, I have come to the conclusion that the lying is ok, because the whole point of video games is to enter a new, fabricated world. This is not the case for a note taking app; in this case, it context is that of a tool. The creativity of the user takes priority. The user does not with to pretend that they are writing on a typewriter; they wish to feel like they are writing on a typewriter. We cannot, however, denie the fact that we are working with a computer interface.

Upon futher writing, the only way inwhich I disagree with Aaron is as follows: he thinks that elegance can only come from function; I thikn that elegance can be the function, but only understand their own nature are elegant. This meanst that a drawing made for the purpose of pure elegance is very elegant, becasue it knows that it is just a drawing. An ornimented piece of furniture is elegant, because it knows that it is a piece of furniture, and it does not try to communicate anything other than that; the ornaments never contradict its nature, but they also do not add to its function, other than by making it more beautiful. Elegance is the result of an object's complete understanding of its own purpose. it is neither*function over form* nor *form over function*; in fact, both are married. The human eye recognizes patterns, and this is what allows us to create stories. All of the words we speak are stories which allow us to communicate something. So to say that using these recognized patterns to embellish designs can only lead to inelegance is wrong I would say. 

According to these thoughts, I would say that the best way to marry the digital and the analogue is to accept the digital as the reality/canvas and the analogue as the tool for story telling. If I think in this way, I will make something elegant. I must not pretend that I am making something real, but I may freely hint at as many real things as I want in order to tell a story. 


I think the reason that the video games bothered me is that I didn't like pretending. I always want everything to feel real and literal. I still feel this way. I refuse to make a theme that looks like a typewriter, because this would go against my core principles that have been there since the very beginning. 

---

I love truthful things. I love truthful things that know how to tell good stories - that know how to move and how to be - that know what they are - who they are.
