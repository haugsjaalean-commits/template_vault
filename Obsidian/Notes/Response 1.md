In *Life After Cable*, XXX presents an unmistakably clear message. The message revolves around a concept that has been around for a millennia: mindfulness and living in the present moment. XXX is convinced that the television takes us further from the very things that make us human. Things like family, human connection, and imagination. That being said, I think that the text leaves a very big question unanswered, and it never acknowledges this fact. Multiple times throughout the text, the author says that consuming televised media distracts us from "real life", as he puts it. This seems very true, especially when he gives examples of the changes in his household and even in the behavior of his children. However, I can't help but feel that these words, "real life", create a central tension in the text. What exactly is "real life" and what is a "distraction"? The author assumes a clear distinction between the two, but I would not be so hasty to imply the existence of such a thing. In order to explain what I mean by this, I would like to talk about my older brother. By his own admission, he is addicted to one of the most popular competitive video games in the world: *League of Legends*. He has described a constant feeling of yearning to play this game when he goas too long without it. His playtime across his multiple accounts surely stretches to many weeks worth of cumulative hours. Hearing about my brother's self proclaimed video game addiction, you, the reader, have probably already developed an opinion about the type of person he is. You probably wouldn't expect him to also be a World Cup gold medalist and Olympic athlete for USA Luge. I mention these facts because I love bragging about my big brother (he's my hero), but also because I think it's important to realize that we often have unchecked biases when it comes to certain things. Things like video games. Things like TV. My brother reads many books, but he says that he never feels more in the present moment than when he plays League of Legends. This is where the difference between *distraction* and *life* becomes very blurry. According to my brother, playing this video game which requires 110% of his concentration is one of the highest states of *[[presentness]]* he can achieve. On the other hand, Thích Nhất Hạnh would seem to say that we are only truly present when we are in complete awareness of our most basic physical existence. ### Thoughts 


I was talking about this text with my parents. I think that verbalizing the point of this written piece helped me further consolidate my ideas. 

Dad talked about how he will often be reading articles before bed when he realizes that he could simply think about his own thoughts instead of someone else's thoughts. This is similar to what the author of the text says about the TV. 

Mom mentioned that the idea that the author arbitrarily judges television as a harmful distraction reminds her of Biron Caty. Biron Caty would ask if we can really know if it's true that TV is a distraction. Can we really know that it's bad for us? My mom says that she knows that TV is a distraction, but she also learns so much from it. For her, it has value in her life. 

---

My goal with this text is to point out what feels to me like a gaping hole in *Life After Cable*. To me, the problem is that it assumes a common understanding of what is "real" and valuable and what is just a harmful "distraction". The author makes claims about these things but has nothing but examples to back them up. My goal is to show that his opinion, which he is trying to pass off as reality, is more contrived than he makes it out to be. It's true that he noticed changes in his own family, but I don't think that gives him the right to make a claim as drastic as "watching television truly does distract a person from real life". This circles back to my thoughts on rhizomes and arborescences. Viewing things in an arborescent manner, we make the assumption that TV is a distraction from life because its affects on people. Viewing things in an rhizomatic manner, we can notice that TV is just one thing that we can focus on among many. We notice the patterns produced when we focus on X thing versus Y thing. We can notice the connexion between X and Y, and we can notice where they diverge. Now that we see things in this way, we can see that the things we had been judging as "real" or "fake" were actually just mental biases created by patterns of thought. If we look into the pattern that created the thought, then we may learn something real. We may learn that there is great value in human connection and imagination. But we may also learn that there is an infinite power in the communication of thoughts by eternalizing them into some piece of media, and that we can learn a great many things from this and be completely expanded. At the same time, we may notice that this power is often misused to create things that seek manipulate our ideas or draw our attention without ever giving anything real in return. We may find that distraction and presentness are actually one in the same, and the difference lies in the nature of the object of focus, and, of course, on the person focusing on it. Some people will want to read a great many books, while others will be satisfied pondering just one or two for a lifetime. 

In any case, looking at things in a more topographical manner will lead us to many valuable insights, but I doubt it will unearth any truths. I am very weary indeed of those who claim to know anything with any certainty. That is my problem with *Life After Cable*. The author seems to think that he knows which distractions are good and which are bad, when what he is really seeing is patterns of behavior - not the truth. It would seem to me that life runs on patterns and not fact or logic.
